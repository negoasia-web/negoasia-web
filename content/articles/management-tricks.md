<p class="lede">By the time you finish reading this article:</p>
<ul>
  <li>You will have ten practical rules to abide by to maximize your negotiations.</li>
  <li>You will know why these rules are important and why they work.</li>
  <li>You will have a one-page negotiation reference sheet to accompany you wherever you go.</li>
  <li>It is highly likely that one idea in this article will challenge your beliefs and change the way you look at negotiations forever.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Rule #1. Negotiate as if your life did not depend on it</h2>
<p>You will do much better if you eliminate the fear associated with life or death thinking in negotiation. In life or death negotiations, the stake is, well, surviving. If you don't reach an agreement, this is the end.</p>
<p>In business negotiations, there are two or more parties engaged in an effort to solve a business issue or to reach a shared objective by means of a series of agreements, and walking away means just that: you have the right to do it, and you will survive.</p>
<p>You will always clear your judgment and decision making by looking at your negotiations, big or small, from some distance.</p>

<h2>Rule #2. No deal is better than a bad deal</h2>
<p>If you don't know what a bad deal looks like to you, how will you know whether the other party or parties have brought you where they wanted you to end up, without you noticing there was something inherently wrong in the deal you craved so much?</p>
<p>Eliminate addiction to instant negotiation gratification — the need to make deals, any deals — by making sure you know and communicate where your boundaries are. Use the tools of assertive communication: persistence, fogging, broken record, negative inquiry about yourself, and &ldquo;I&rdquo; messages. No, &ldquo;I&rdquo; is not evil: it is a strong way to communicate feelings, perceptions, and, as importantly, what you want.</p>
<p>Invite all parties to tell you what a bad deal looks like to them. You will learn a lot, because people tend to talk more when they have negative things to communicate. And the more they talk, the more they will disclose what is important to them, which is the key to unlock your negotiations.</p>

<h2>Rule #3. Have a written mission &amp; purpose</h2>
<p>Even in the most adversarial negotiations, all parties are at the table because they feel they need someone from the other parties in order to solve a problem or reach an objective which they cannot attain on their own.</p>
<p>What is it that you have that can bring long term value to them? Under which conditions are you willing to contribute it to create a virtuous, sustainable cycle of agreements which can benefit you as you are contributing to the outcomes they are looking for?</p>
<p>Writing it down will prevent you from confusing unnecessary and forced compromise — to reach the appearance of a deal — with necessary and voluntary contribution to the co-construction of a robust agreement which will stand the test of time. It will become your negotiation True North, against which you can evaluate all your decisions. It will be the foundation of your negotiation leadership.</p>
<p>No wind is a good wind if you don't know where you're going. Define, refine, your destination, write it down, then do what it takes to make it happen.</p>

<h2>Rule #4. Empty your cup</h2>
<p>A few hundred years ago, a young monk came to see a wise old sage on the top of the Himalayas to seek enlightenment over tea. The old sage started pouring the finest tea in existence in the whole region, rich in unknown flavors and scents, in the young monk's cup. All the while, the young monk explained to the old sage all the things he had learned, all the things he knew, all the understanding he had reached about worldly and heavenly affairs.</p>
<p>While the young monk was speaking, the old sage kept pouring tea in the young monk's cup, so much so that it eventually overflowed. Seeing that the old sage continued pouring tea in the now overflowing cup, the young monk interjected: &ldquo;What are you doing? Can't you see the cup is full?&rdquo; — to which the old sage replied: &ldquo;There are few objects as useful as an empty cup.&rdquo; And at that, he stood up, and left.</p>
<p>Empty your cup. Forget what you think you know, forget what you think you understand. Clear your mind and create an empty space ready to receive what you don't know, what you don't understand. There is power in ignorance. Use it.</p>

<h2>Rule #5. Empathy comes first</h2>
<p>If it comes first, why is it not even on the last step of the rules podium? Because the risk is high to misunderstand the empathy concept if you embrace it without distance, without boundaries, and without an empty mind which protects you from the neediness born from the confirmation bias created by your expectations.</p>
<p>In high stakes negotiations, there is a very high likelihood that all parties at the table do not have your interests at heart, but their own. And it all makes sense, because negotiation is not mediation. So you need to look at your coming negotiation from a coaching distance — what psychologists call the &ldquo;meta&rdquo; position — and know your limits before you engage.</p>
<p>You are at the table because &ldquo;they&rdquo; need something from you. So empathy is number one in the sense that only by empathizing with them will you create a safe environment in which they can faithfully disclose who they are and what they want. In other words: use emotional intelligence to create the conditions for a safe exchange of information.</p>

<h2>Rule #6. Ask awesome questions</h2>
<p>Now that your cup is (hopefully) empty, that you have made empathy a priority after making sure you know your limits, which can frame the conversation because your life does not depend on it — now you must ask questions. Let me rephrase that. You must ask <strong>awesome</strong> questions.</p>
<p>What is an awesome question? To answer that, it might be a good idea to ask ourselves what a question is. A question is not a sentence with a question mark at the end. Ninety percent of questions asked on autopilot are actually just assumptions or interpretations followed by a question mark. &ldquo;You said this… is it because…?&rdquo; is one example.</p>
<p>Remember that emptying your cup means no expectations. Expectations trigger the confirmation bias, which prevents you from asking awesome questions and makes it impossible to hear the answers which contradict them. Furthermore, expectations fuel neediness, the negotiation failure disease, and the fear — False Expectations Appearing Real — of losing &ldquo;the&rdquo; deal.</p>
<blockquote>I keep six honest serving-men (they taught me all I knew); their names are What and Why and When and How and Where and Who.<cite style="display:block;font-family:'Libre Franklin',sans-serif;font-weight:400;font-style:normal;font-size:.85rem;color:var(--muted);margin-top:.7rem">Rudyard Kipling</cite></blockquote>
<p>An awesome question serves several purposes. The first, obvious to everybody, is to get answers. The second, hidden to most, is to direct the brain of the people you ask toward their vision of the business or personal pain they are trying to avoid, or the objective they want to achieve and the pain of not being there yet — and how what you bring to the table can help them get from here to there.</p>

<h2>Rule #7. Listen!</h2>
<blockquote>Seek first to understand, then to be understood.<cite style="display:block;font-family:'Libre Franklin',sans-serif;font-weight:400;font-style:normal;font-size:.85rem;color:var(--muted);margin-top:.7rem">Stephen Covey</cite></blockquote>
<p>I am talking about active listening, deep listening, focused listening. The kind of listening in which you hear only one voice, that of the person speaking, not yours. By this I mean shut down that internal voice which is telling you what to say next while the person opposite the table is speaking.</p>
<p>Listen with the intent of discovering what the real problem is, what the hidden key to the negotiation is, as seen by the person who is talking. Listen to the content, but also to how they see the world. Are they visual, auditive, kinesthetic? Focused on the past, the present, or the future? Do they speak more about people, activities, things, ideas, places? Are they detail-oriented or big picture?</p>
<p>Listen to what they are saying about their vision of pain, defined as the driver to the negotiation, and the systems at work for or against them as well as for or against you.</p>
<p>And take great notes.</p>

<h2>Rule #8. Take great notes</h2>
<p>Enough that you may keep the core of what was said. Little enough that you don't miss what is being said because you are too busy noting. If you can, assign one team member as a note taker so you can listen more and better.</p>
<p>What does taking great notes mean? Your notes must answer the questions below.</p>
<ul>
  <li>What pain did they acknowledge that I can alleviate?</li>
  <li>What objective haven't they reached that I can help them reach?</li>
  <li>What did they say, what didn't they say?</li>
  <li>What problems do I see, what opportunities, how will I test them?</li>
  <li>How do they make decisions?</li>
  <li>How do they see value?</li>
  <li>How invested are the various decision makers in terms of time, energy, money, and emotion?</li>
  <li>How do I know that?</li>
  <li>What have we achieved? What is missing? Where is this going?</li>
</ul>
<p>These kinds of notes.</p>

<h2>Rule #9. Prepare and execute valid negotiation checklists</h2>
<p>You are going to be subject to strong winds and sudden weather changes, but you must go on flying the plane. What do pilots rely on? An overall flight plan — your negotiator's Mission &amp; Purpose — and checklists to guide them through tough weather.</p>
<p>What does a negotiation checklist look like? There are several schools of thought, so these are suggestions you can adapt to your background and your negotiations, because we are all different and no two negotiations are identical. A great checklist includes the answers to the following questions.</p>
<ul>
  <li>Who is sitting on the opposite side of the (possibly virtual) table?</li>
  <li>Who is deciding behind the scenes?</li>
  <li>How will they see and decide what quality and quantity of value I bring to the table?</li>
  <li>What power games could they use?</li>
  <li>What are their and our alternatives? What happens on both sides if we do not reach agreement?</li>
  <li>What do we want to collectively achieve? What are my boundaries?</li>
  <li>What are the negotiating points on my side? On their side?</li>
  <li>What does the zone of possible agreement — the collaborative value — look like? What do the tradeables look like?</li>
  <li>What are the problems I have identified which block the path to an agreement? Are there emotional obstacles?</li>
  <li>What questions will I ask, and how will I ask them to create the right climate for truthful answers?</li>
  <li>What happens after what comes next?</li>
</ul>

<h2>Rule #10. Proper preparation does not prevent poor performance</h2>
<p>Don't let your obsession with results ruin your focus on preparation. Don't let your need to please ruin your focus on execution.</p>
<p>Focus on what you can control: your activities — what you do; your behaviors — what you say and how you say it; your decisions. If you try to control the uncontrollable, the uncontrollable will control you.</p>
<p>You cannot control time, places, people, the past, and most importantly you cannot control results — only what is under your control to navigate a path toward accomplishment of your mission and purpose. Influence is not control.</p>
<p>Instead of trying to control the uncontrollable, execute as planned. Remember Top Gun's motto: fight as we train. Proper preparation which is not followed by proper execution is an exercise in futility.</p>

<h2>Rule #11. Create a state of flow</h2>
<p>Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi describes flow as the state of mind in which you are at the same time wide awake and aware of everything happening around you in a particular context, while being able to adapt to the circumstances according to a playbook you have defined in advance.</p>
<p>To create this state of flow in your negotiations, you have to integrate all the above elements into a system. Your well-taken notes become the foundation of your checklists for the next negotiation event, in which you apply the preceding rules to move toward the accomplishment of your mission and purpose, to the long term benefit of the other party or parties, within the limits of your boundaries.</p>
